How much to charge for bookkeeping: work out your rate, your monthly fees and a price list

Most bookkeepers set their first prices by looking at what someone else charges, knocking a bit off, and then quietly doing far more work than the fee covers. This guide gives you a method that starts from your own numbers instead: the hourly rate you actually need, a fixed monthly fee for each client built from the work they really create, a catch-up quote that doesn't lose money, and a price list you can publish. The free calculator does the sums.

Updated 28 October 2026 · UK and US · Cost figures checked against gov.uk · General information, not business or tax advice

In one paragraph: price from time and a target rate, then sell a fixed fee. Add the income you want to your yearly overheads and divide by the hours you can really bill (usually 60–70% of your working hours, not 100%). That's your target hourly rate. For each client, add up the minutes their month takes (transactions, reconciliations, bills, VAT, payroll, emails), add an allowance for messy records and a contingency, multiply by your rate and round up. Quote it as a fixed monthly fee with what it includes and what changes it. Price catch-up work separately, with written assumptions and a deposit.

Step 1: work out the hourly rate you need

A price is only "right" if it pays for your life and your business after the hours you can't bill. The sum is short:

Target hourly rate = (income you want + yearly overheads) ÷ billable hours a year

The figure most people get wrong is billable hours. A full-time year is not 52 × 37.5 = 1,950 hours of client work. Take off holidays, bank holidays and sick days (46 working weeks is a fair planning figure), then take off the hours that are real work but nobody pays for: answering enquiries, writing proposals, chasing records, software updates, CPD, your own bookkeeping, and the first month of every new client while you learn their books. For a solo practice, 60–70% billable is a common planning figure; in your first year, while you're still finding clients, it can be much lower.

Worked exampleFigure
Income wanted before tax£36,000
Yearly overheads£4,000
Billable hours: 46 weeks × 37.5 hours × 65%1,121 hours
Target rate: £40,000 ÷ 1,121£35.67, call it £36 an hour

The same £40,000 spread over all 1,725 working hours (46 × 37.5) would suggest £23 an hour. That gap is why bookkeepers who price from a "full-time hours" sum end up working evenings for less than they planned. If the rate you get looks higher than your local market will pay, the answer is rarely to drop it: it's to get faster per client (bank rules, receipt apps, records-due dates), specialise in a kind of client you can serve quickly, or plan a smaller income for year one and review it.

Use the "Check an existing client" box in the calculator for clients you already have: annual fee ÷ hours spent gives their effective rate. Anyone well under your target is either under-priced or has grown since you set the fee.

Step 2: turn each client's workload into a fixed monthly fee

Clients pay for outcomes, but your time goes on volume. A fee that ignores volume is a guess. These are the things that drive the hours:

  • Transactions a month, across every bank, card and payment account (Stripe, PayPal, Square, Amazon, eBay payouts all count). This is the single biggest driver.
  • Accounts to reconcile. Each extra account is another reconciliation every month, whatever its volume.
  • Bills, receipts and sales invoices that need entering, matching or checking.
  • Returns and runs: VAT (quarterly or monthly), payroll (per run and per employee), CIS.
  • Reporting: a monthly report or management accounts takes real time if you write commentary.
  • How the records arrive. The same 80 transactions take far longer when receipts come in a carrier bag in week four than when they're uploaded to an app as they happen. Charge for that difference, or use it to change the habit.
  • Emails and questions. "Unlimited support" on a small fee is where many practices lose money. Allow for it, and say in the engagement letter what's included.

Most small practices end up with fixed monthly packages for routine work, fixed quotes for projects, and an hourly rate only for the genuinely unknown.

Pricing catch-up (clean-up) bookkeeping

Catch-up jobs are where bookkeepers lose the most money, because the client always says "it's not that bad" and the box always contains more than expected. Our bookkeeping cleanup checklist builds the scope (the statements to request, the deadlines at stake and the order of work). Then price it as a fixed project with assumptions:

  1. Scope it before you quote. Ask for read access to the bank feeds or a statement for one month, and count. Months behind × transactions a month × accounts is the base.
  2. Add a contingency by what exists. As a starting point: about 20% if full statements and most receipts exist, 40% if documents are missing, 70% or more if statements still need requesting from the bank. Change these once you've done a few.
  3. Write the assumptions into the quote: the period, the accounts, the transaction count with a margin, how quickly the client answers queries, and what isn't included (earlier periods, HMRC letters, tax returns).
  4. Take a deposit (many take 50%) before you start, and the balance on completion, before or alongside handing over.
  5. Offer the monthly package at the end, so they never need a catch-up again.

The calculator's step 3 builds the quote text. With its defaults (6 months, 120 transactions a month, 2 accounts, 2 VAT returns, receipts missing, £36 an hour) it comes to 26.6 hours and a fixed fee of £975 with a £490 deposit.

A bookkeeping price list template

A published price list saves you writing every quote from scratch and filters out clients who only want the cheapest option. A simple structure that works:

  • Three packages by monthly transaction band, e.g. up to 50, 51–150 and 151–300, each with a stated number of bank accounts.
  • Add-ons priced separately: VAT returns, payroll (a base price plus per employee), CIS, extra accounts, every 50 transactions over the band, catch-up months.
  • "From" prices or exact prices. "From" is honest when records vary; say what moves the price.
  • What isn't included: year-end accounts and tax returns (unless you do them), HMRC enquiries, software subscriptions. If you do Self Assessment returns, price them separately: the Self Assessment fee guide has a per-page calculator.
  • VAT: say "plus VAT" if you're registered.

Step 4 of the calculator builds the bands and add-ons from your rate and times and downloads them as a CSV for Excel or Google Sheets. Sense-check the result against what bookkeepers near you publish: if yours is far higher, look at your times; if it's far lower, look at your billable percentage.

Seven pricing mistakes that cost bookkeepers the most

  1. Pricing from 100% billable hours. It sets your rate about a third too low before you've started.
  2. No volume in the scope. "Monthly bookkeeping" with no transaction or account limit means the fee never moves while the work doubles.
  3. Unlimited emails and calls. Include a fair amount and say so; offer a package with a monthly call for clients who want more.
  4. Free catch-up "to win the client". The first months are the hardest. Charge for them, or the client learns the work is worth nothing.
  5. A first-year discount with no end date. If you discount, write the date the normal fee starts.
  6. Never reviewing fees. Review every client once a year against hours and volume, and give at least 30 days' notice (or what your engagement letter says) of any change.
  7. Doing the "quick extra job" before quoting it. Reply with the price first: "Happy to help. That's outside your package, so it would be £60, or I can add it to your monthly fee. Shall I go ahead?"

Raising fees for existing clients

Use the effective-rate check to sort clients from lowest to highest, then treat them in groups: a short notice letter for clients who are roughly right, a letter with the numbers ("we now reconcile about 400 transactions a month, up from 150 when we agreed your fee") for those who've grown, package options for those asking for more than they pay for, and a polite handover for the few who are unprofitable and won't change. Give the new fee, the date, what's included and who to call. No apologies, no fake deadlines.

Using AI to help price

AI chat tools are good at structure and wording, and unreliable on market data. A prompt that works:

I'm a self-employed bookkeeper. My target rate is £36 an hour. My task times are: 1 minute per bank line, 15 minutes per account reconciliation, 2 minutes per bill, 60 minutes per quarterly VAT return.

A new client (client code C14) has about 120 bank lines a month across 2 accounts, 25 bills, quarterly VAT and no payroll. Records usually arrive late.

1. List every monthly task with the minutes, and total them. Show the sums.
2. Add 15% for late records and 10% contingency, and give a fixed monthly fee rounded up to the next £5.
3. Write the "what's included" lines and the assumptions for the engagement letter.
4. Write a short, friendly proposal email. Don't mention other firms' prices or claim anything about the market.
  • Don't ask it what bookkeepers charge. It may produce confident, made-up "average rates".
  • Check every sum. Chat tools can multiply wrongly; the calculator above is a quick cross-check.
  • Use a client code, not the client's name, and leave out bank details.

FAQ

How much should I charge for bookkeeping?

Work it out rather than copying someone else's number. Add the income you want to your yearly overheads (software, insurance, money laundering supervision, membership, training), divide by the hours you can really bill (often 60 to 70% of your working hours), and that's your target hourly rate. Then time each client's monthly work and quote a fixed fee of time multiplied by that rate, rounded up. The free calculator does each step.

Should I charge bookkeeping clients hourly or a fixed monthly fee?

Most small-business clients prefer a fixed monthly fee because they know the cost in advance, and it rewards you for getting faster. Work it out from time and your target rate, write down what it includes (transactions, accounts, returns), and say what changes it. Hourly billing still suits one-off advice, messy catch-up work you can't scope yet, and clients whose volume swings wildly.

How do I price catch-up or clean-up bookkeeping?

Count the months behind, the transactions per month and the accounts, estimate the time, then add a contingency for missing records: around 20% if full statements and most receipts exist, 40% if documents are missing, and more if statements still need requesting. Quote one fixed fee with the assumptions written down, take a deposit before starting, and quote anything outside the assumptions separately before doing it.

What are the average bookkeeping rates in the UK?

Published figures vary widely by region, experience, software and client type, and many are out of date or from firms selling a service. A rate copied from someone else won't cover your costs and hours. Work out your own target rate first, then compare it with the published price lists of bookkeepers near you and any fee survey your professional body publishes.

Do I need to add VAT to my bookkeeping fees?

Only if you're VAT-registered. In the UK you must register once your taxable turnover goes over £90,000 in a rolling 12 months, and then you add VAT (usually 20%) to your fees. Clients who aren't VAT-registered can't reclaim it, so for them your price effectively rises by a fifth. Plan for that before you reach the threshold.

Can I use ChatGPT to work out my bookkeeping prices?

It's useful for structure: listing a client's tasks, drafting a proposal, writing the fee-increase letter. Don't ask it what bookkeepers charge, because it can invent market rates. Give it your own target rate and task times, check every sum it does, and don't paste client names or bank details.
